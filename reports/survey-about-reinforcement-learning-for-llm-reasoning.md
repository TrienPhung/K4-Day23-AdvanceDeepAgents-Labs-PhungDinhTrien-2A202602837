# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Methods, Evidence, and Open Problems

## TL;DR
- RL is a post-training stage that can optimize a language model against preference feedback or task-level correctness, complementing demonstration-based supervised fine-tuning [1][2].
- Recent reasoning systems use verifiable reward signals and policy optimization to encourage multi-step problem solving; DeepSeekMath presents GRPO, while DeepSeek-R1 reports a multi-stage pipeline and an RL-only starting point [3][4][5].
- Process supervision gives feedback on intermediate steps, whereas outcome supervision grades the final answer; these signals provide different trade-offs in credit assignment and reward design [2][6].
- Results depend on what is measured: reasoning benchmarks cover math, code and science, but coding contamination controls, test-time sampling and domain-specific evaluation affect interpretation [7][5][8][9][10].
- The central unresolved issue is generalization beyond tasks with reliable verifiers, alongside the risk that optimization exploits reward weaknesses instead of learning robust reasoning [11][12][13].

## Background
Here, reinforcement learning (RL) for LLM reasoning means post-training a language model so its generated solution trajectories receive higher expected reward. The reward can reflect human judgments, a learned reward model, or an automatically checked outcome. It is distinct from pretraining, which learns broad language patterns, and from supervised fine-tuning (SFT), which imitates example responses. RL is useful when desired behavior can be evaluated more directly than it can be exhaustively demonstrated, or when the model can improve by exploring alternative solution paths. This framing does not establish that generated chains of thought are equivalent to human or symbolic reasoning; contemporary post-training surveys explicitly caution against that inference [14].

Instruct-following work describes supervised fine-tuning on labeler demonstrations followed by reinforcement learning from human feedback using output rankings [1]. Reasoning work shifts emphasis from broad preference to task success and trace quality. “Let’s Verify Step by Step” contrasts outcome supervision (grading a final result) with process supervision (labeling individual reasoning steps) and reports stronger MATH-task performance for process supervision in its study [2]. More recently, public descriptions of o1 associate large-scale RL with improved complex reasoning and report gains from both RL training compute and additional inference-time thinking [7]. These results frame reasoning not only as a model capability but as a training-and-compute regime.

## Reward signals and policy optimization
A defining design choice is what the policy is rewarded for. Outcome reward models score the answer or task completion; process reward models (PRMs) score intermediate steps, potentially furnishing denser feedback for long solutions. Human step labels, as in PRM800K, can make local errors visible, while a final-answer checker is cheaper to apply at scale when answers are formally verifiable [2]. These rewards are not interchangeable: the former needs reliable judgments about each step, while the latter may not reveal where a trajectory went wrong.

The move from PPO-style RLHF to reasoning-oriented policy optimization illustrates a computational trade-off. The RRHF paper describes the standard pipeline as SFT, reward-model training and PPO, and notes PPO sensitivity to hyperparameters and the burden of multiple models; it proposes ranking responses as an alternative [15]. DeepSeekMath introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as part of its approach to mathematical reasoning, using groups of sampled responses to provide relative policy feedback [3]. DeepSeek-R1 reports reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards alongside multi-stage training, and R1-Zero is presented as an RL-trained system without preliminary SFT [4][5]. Together, these works signal a shift toward using task-checkable reward and sampled solution diversity, rather than relying only on a separately learned preference model. The sources here do not establish that one optimizer dominates across model scales or task domains.

Reward granularity introduces its own optimization problem. The PURE paper argues that conventional summation-form credit assignment with PRMs can reward high-scoring steps in a way that invites reward hacking, and proposes a min-form alternative [6]. Thus, richer process feedback can improve diagnosis of intermediate errors but also creates more opportunities for the policy to exploit imperfections in step scoring. Prolonged RL training with verifiable rewards is another explored axis; one study describes enhanced GRPO and reports improvements on math, coding and logic tasks [16]. The available findings make training duration and reward construction active design variables, not simply implementation details.

## From benchmark gains to measured reasoning
Published evaluations span mathematical problem solving, coding, scientific question answering and broader applications. DeepSeek-R1’s report evaluates math and code benchmarks as well as GPQA and software engineering tasks, illustrating the breadth of claims now attached to reasoning models [5]. OpenAI’s o1 announcement similarly presents math, coding and graduate-level science results, while reporting substantial differences between single-sample and high-compute sampling settings [7]. Therefore, a headline score should be read with the test-time procedure in view: repeated sampling or reranking measures a system using additional inference compute, not just a single policy completion [7].

Benchmarks themselves determine what “success” means. LiveCodeBench continuously collects new programming problems and uses release and model-cutoff dates to reduce contamination; its authors also caution against overinterpreting small score differences [8]. GPQA consists of expert-written graduate-level biology, physics and chemistry questions and was designed to be difficult even for skilled non-experts with web access [9]. In clinical decision support, an expert-rated study compared DeepSeek-R1 and other models on 125 patient cases, underscoring that domain-specific evaluation can use expert judgments rather than exact-match answer keys [10]. R-Bench likewise targets multidisciplinary, graduate-level and cross-lingual reasoning settings [17]. These forms of assessment are complementary: exact verification is scalable for formal answers, but expert review is needed for some open-ended or high-stakes outputs.

## Verifiability, generalization, and reward reliability
RL with verifiable rewards has been especially attractive in mathematics and code because a checker can determine whether an answer or program passes a defined criterion. The boundary is important: rule-based checking does not naturally extend to many domains, including healthcare, law and biology, while substituting an LLM verifier raises reliance on a strong verifier [11]. In such settings, binary correctness may be unavailable or may fail to capture whether the reasoning is sound, safe or useful.

Verifier reliability also limits performance within nominally checkable tasks. RL Tango identifies fixed rule-based or frozen verifiers as vulnerable to reward hacking and poor generalization beyond their training distributions, and proposes jointly reinforcing generator and verifier [12]. A separate line of work proposes co-optimizing policy and reward models to improve robustness and mitigate reward hacking [18]. These approaches respond to an incentive problem: policy optimization can amplify small systematic errors in reward signals. They are promising research directions, but the summaries available do not establish that joint optimization fully eliminates reward exploitation.

Generalization is not guaranteed by benchmark improvement. A recent analysis of mathematical case studies warns that RLVR gains on combinatorial problems can reinforce superficial heuristics rather than transferable reasoning strategies [13]. This distinction calls for evaluation on shifted problem distributions and on the intermediate behavior that produces an answer, not only held-out scores. Calibration is another weakly served objective: RLCR uses calibration rewards to target both answer accuracy and confidence calibration [19]. The inclusion of confidence as a reward dimension suggests that correctness alone is insufficient where models must communicate uncertainty.

## Trends and open problems
Over the recent wave of reasoning-focused RL, the evidence points to three connected changes: reward has moved toward automatically verifiable task outcomes; training recipes increasingly combine RL with staged data and optimization; and inference-time sampling or interaction has become part of the reported capability [7][4][16]. Yet those changes make comparisons harder, because results can reflect different training mixtures, reward definitions and inference budgets rather than a single improvement in reasoning quality.

A second trend is extending beyond static “think then answer” behavior. Work on test-time interaction argues that interactive agents need to act to acquire new environmental information, and proposes scaling the interaction horizon as a distinct dimension from longer reasoning traces [20]. This raises open questions about when a model should deliberate, use tools, explore, or stop; it also requires evaluation that includes adaptation over multiple actions.

The field still needs stronger evidence on transfer, verifier robustness, and data and compute efficiency. Data-efficient policy optimization such as DEPO explicitly targets offline and online data selection to reduce cost while improving performance [21], but the broader question is how much useful reasoning is acquired per unit of data and compute. For open-ended domains, reliable reward construction remains unresolved; for formally verifiable tasks, shortcut learning and distribution shift remain concerns [11][13]. Finally, benchmark scores should be reported with transparent sampling and compute protocols and paired with contamination-aware, expert-reviewed, or interactive evaluations where relevant [7][8][10][20]. The most consequential test is not merely whether RL raises scores on the verifier’s distribution, but whether learned strategies remain useful when tasks, evaluators and environments change.
