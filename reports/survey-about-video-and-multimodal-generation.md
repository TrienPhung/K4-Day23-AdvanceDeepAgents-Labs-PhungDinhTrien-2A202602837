# Video and Multimodal Generation: Methods, Control, and Evaluation

## TL;DR
- Video generation has moved from autoregressive prediction over compressed discrete codes toward diffusion in compact video latents, increasingly using Transformer-based spatiotemporal architectures; these families trade sequence modeling choices against computational cost and temporal structure.[1][2][3]
- The current design space is not just text-to-video: image, motion, trajectory, interactive editing, and subject-specific conditions provide finer control, while audio-video work seeks temporal as well as semantic alignment.[4][5][6][7]
- Scaling is a multidimensional challenge: latent compression and windowed attention reduce modeling burden, but long-horizon consistency and fidelity remain explicit concerns in the literature.[3][8][9]
- Evaluation is broadening from a single notion of visual quality to dimension-specific faithfulness, physics, identity consistency, naturalness, and safety.[10][11][12][13]

## Background
Video and multimodal generation concerns synthesizing temporally ordered visual content, often conditioned on text, images, motion, or other signals, and increasingly generating or coordinating sound with video. Unlike still-image synthesis, a model must represent both appearance and change over time; conditioning adds a further requirement that generated events track user intent. The sources here span text-to-video, image-to-video, editing, customized subject generation, and joint or cross-modal audio-video tasks.[3][4][5][14][7]

A useful historical starting point is VideoGPT, which learns a 3D-convolutional VQ-VAE to encode video as discrete codes and then uses a GPT-like Transformer to model those codes autoregressively. The authors motivate the compressed representation by video’s spatial and temporal redundancy, and report competitive results on BAIR Robot alongside natural-video samples on UCF-101 and TGIF.[1] Video diffusion instead extends denoising-based generation to sequences; one early formulation trains jointly on images and videos, reporting reduced minibatch-gradient variance and conditional techniques for longer or higher-resolution output.[2] A later survey describes the progression from 3D U-Nets through latent diffusion toward Transformer-based latent video diffusion, emphasizing reduced-dimensional representations and more explicit temporal modeling.[3] The shift is therefore not a simple replacement of one architecture: it is a movement in where video is represented and how space-time dependencies are computed.

## From compressed sequences to spatiotemporal diffusion

The architectural tension is between expressive sequence modeling and the cost of processing dense video. VideoGPT addresses this by compressing frames into discrete tokens before autoregressive prediction.[1] Diffusion methods formulate generation as iterative denoising, and joint image/video training offers a way to use both modalities within one training setup.[2] Latent video diffusion further reduces the representation to make synthesis less expensive, while retaining a temporal model over the compressed sequence.[3]

Transformer-based systems make this efficiency–structure trade-off especially visible. W.A.L.T uses a causal encoder to map images and videos into a shared lower-dimensional latent space, then alternates spatial and spatiotemporal window attention rather than applying global attention everywhere.[8] Wan is described as combining a Diffusion Transformer with flow matching, text cross-attention, full spatiotemporal attention, and a causal spatiotemporal VAE; its tasks include image-to-video and instruction-guided editing.[9] These examples represent different strategies: W.A.L.T emphasizes windowed attention and shared image/video compression, whereas Wan’s description emphasizes a compressed video tokenizer and full space-time attention. Both make latent representation central, but the cited material does not establish a direct apples-to-apples efficiency or quality ranking.[8][9]

## Conditioning, control, and user interaction

Text provides a broad semantic direction, but more explicit controls aim to specify what moves, how a camera behaves, or which subject should persist. InteractiveVideo treats generation as an iterative user-facing process with painting, drag-and-drop, and text/image instructions; its full-paper description also includes image, text, motion, and trajectory conditions.[4] Motion Prompting instead centers trajectories as control signals for dynamic actions and temporal composition, including camera control and motion transfer.[6] Together, these approaches expose a useful distinction: interaction lets a user revise or localize intent during generation, while trajectory conditioning encodes a more structured temporal plan.[4][6]

Customized generation adds identity as another control axis. HunyuanCustom combines text, image, audio, and video conditions for subject-consistent generation, using text-image fusion and identity enhancement according to its summary.[5] These methods suggest that “controllability” is not one capability: spatial editing, motion specification, and identity persistence demand different kinds of condition representations and different evaluation targets.[4][5][6]

Audio-video generation makes cross-modal alignment an explicit modeling problem. MultiFoley generates Foley audio with video, text, or reference-audio conditions, including silent video with text; the paper reports full-bandwidth 48 kHz sound and synchronized output.[14] AV-Link seeks bidirectional video-to-audio and audio-to-video generation by conditioning frozen diffusion models on temporally aligned features from the other modality; the authors report improved synchronization, including onset-accuracy gains over a baseline.[7] SAVA takes a different, cascaded route for text-to-audio-video generation, using adapted latent components to address incomplete or asymmetric correspondence between modalities.[15] The contrast is between direct cross-modal feature exchange and a staged generation path: both target synchronization, but their architectures place coordination at different points in the pipeline.[7][15]

## Scaling quality, duration, and temporal coherence

Scaling should be understood as several coupled demands rather than a single parameter-count problem. Higher resolution and longer duration increase the volume of spatiotemporal information a model must represent; compact latents and localized attention reduce some of that burden.[3][8] The surveyed literature identifies long-video mechanisms as a continuing direction, while W.A.L.T’s window attention offers a concrete way to avoid unrestricted global interactions.[3][8] At the same time, methods that constrain attention or compress representations must preserve temporal details and visual fidelity; efficiency alone is not a sufficient criterion for a usable generator.[3][8]

Control can also help define what “consistency” means. Persistent subject identity is a target in customized video generation, while motion trajectories and camera prompts constrain temporal composition.[5][6] Audio-video systems add synchronization between sound events and visible events as a separate temporal criterion.[14][7] This plurality matters: a video can look plausible frame by frame yet fail to preserve a subject, follow a requested motion, or align its soundtrack. The source set does not provide a common benchmark comparing these systems across duration, resolution, and control, so their reported capabilities should not be treated as directly comparable.[5][6][7]

## Evaluation, datasets, and safety

Evaluation is moving toward decomposed criteria. VBench++ argues that existing metrics may not align sufficiently with human perception and organizes quality into hierarchical, disentangled dimensions with tailored prompts and evaluation methods.[10] VBench-2.0 focuses on intrinsic faithfulness and lists human fidelity, controllability, creativity, physics, and commonsense among its evaluation dimensions.[11] For subject-to-video specifically, OpenS2V-Nexus targets subject consistency and naturalness, illustrating why a general quality score may conceal task-specific failures.[12] These efforts complement rather than replace one another: broad suites diagnose multiple properties, while focused benchmarks test whether a particular condition—such as subject identity—is obeyed.

Data and safety also shape evaluation. TIP-I2V supplies over 1.70 million user-provided text-and-image prompts for image-to-video research, addressing the lack of a dedicated prompt dataset described by its authors.[16] Safety benchmarks identify a different gap: Video-SafetyBench argues that static-image safety tests can miss risks tied to video’s temporal dynamics, and introduces 2,264 video-text pairs across 48 unsafe categories.[13] T2VSafetyBench focuses specifically on text-to-video safety and highlights a safety–usability trade-off.[17] Together, these sources imply that benchmark coverage must reflect both how people prompt video systems and risks that emerge across time—not just properties of isolated frames.[16][13][17]

## Trends and open problems

Recent work in this source set broadens generation along three connected axes: latent Transformer architectures, richer user control, and outputs that coordinate video with audio.[8][9][4][7] In parallel, benchmarks increasingly ask whether models follow instructions faithfully, represent plausible physics, preserve identity, and meet safety expectations rather than only whether frames appear realistic.[10][11][12] A central unresolved issue is how to compare systems across these criteria: the notes provide diverse benchmarks and task-specific claims, but no shared evaluation spanning long duration, multimodal synchronization, identity, and safety.[10][13][12]

Several problems follow directly from this evidence. First, temporal coherence is multi-faceted: identity, motion, event timing, and audio synchronization are related but non-equivalent objectives.[5][6][7] Second, efficiency techniques should be evaluated for quality as well as speed or compactness, since architectural savings do not alone establish fidelity.[3][8] Third, safety assessment needs video-aware temporal tests while also measuring the safety–usability balance.[13][17] Progress will therefore depend not only on larger generators, but on representations and benchmarks that make these different forms of control, coherence, and risk measurable.
